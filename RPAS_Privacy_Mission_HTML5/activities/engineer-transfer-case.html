Case Study

Real Estate
Filming Incident

A real-world scenario exploring what happens when a commercial drone job goes wrong — and the legal consequences that follow.

📋 Incident Summary

A drone operator was commissioned by a real estate agent to capture aerial footage of a residential property for marketing purposes.

During the flight, the operator inadvertently filmed neighbouring backyards, capturing clear images of private property — including identifiable people — without those residents' knowledge or consent.

The real estate agent subsequently published the footage online as part of a property listing. A neighbouring homeowner recognised their yard in the footage and lodged a formal complaint.

How It Unfolded

1
The Commission Real estate agent hires drone operator to film a residential property listing.
2
The Flight Operator flies the drone and inadvertently captures neighbouring backyards in the footage.
3
The Publication Raw footage is reviewed and published online without editing out neighbouring properties or people.
4
The Complaint A neighbour identifies their property and lodges a formal complaint against the real estate agent and the drone operator.

Legal Analysis

Legal Breach
Risk Exposure

Two distinct liability tracks — regulatory and civil — with different triggers and different parties exposed.

🏛️
Track 1 — APP Entities · Regulatory

Privacy Act 1988 — OAIC Enforcement

Where the operator or agency is an APP entity (annual turnover >$3M), the OAIC may issue a compliance notice, an infringement notice (up to $66K for individuals), or commence proceedings in the Federal Court for serious interference with privacy — under the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024.

⚖️
Track 2 — All Operators · Civil Action

Statutory Tort — Serious Invasion of Privacy

Even where the operator is not an APP entity, the property owner may sue directly under the new statutory tort (commenced 10 June 2025). The tort applies to any individual or organisation. The owner must show: intrusion upon their seclusion, a reasonable expectation of privacy, and that the invasion was intentional or reckless. No proof of damage required.

💡

"I didn't mean to film them" is not a defence. Filming neighbouring properties without a site assessment — especially where footage is published — is likely to meet the recklessness threshold. Both tracks can run simultaneously.

Practical Guidance

Implications for
Drone Pilots

What this incident means for your operations — and the steps every commercial drone pilot should take before, during and after every job.

⚠️

This Is Not Hypothetical

A near-identical incident occurred in Victoria, where a woman sunbathing in her backyard was accidentally filmed by a drone commissioned by a neighbouring real estate firm for a property listing. The legal and reputational consequences were significant for all parties involved.

What You Must Do

✓
Conduct a Pre-Flight Site Assessment Before every commercial job, identify all neighbouring properties within your flight path. Assess whether your planned trajectory will capture adjacent yards, pools, or occupied areas — and adjust your flight plan accordingly.
✓
Confirm Your Licensing and Insurance Commercial operations require a valid ReOC (Remote Operator Certificate) or operation under the excluded category. Ensure you carry public liability insurance — unregulated operators are not required to hold it, but the risk is entirely yours if you don't.
✓
Separate Raw and Published Footage Do not hand over unreviewed raw footage to a client. Review all footage first and advise the client to edit out any frames that capture neighbouring properties, identifiable individuals, or private activity before publication.
✓
Use Written Service Agreements Your contract with the client should clearly define responsibilities for footage review, publication decisions, and compliance with privacy laws. Don't assume the real estate agent understands their obligations — document yours.
✓
Know Your State's Surveillance Laws Privacy and surveillance obligations vary by state. In NSW, the Surveillance Devices Act 2007 is the key instrument. In Victoria, relevant provisions focus on recording private activities. Queensland, Tasmania, and the ACT currently have more limited protections. Know the rules where you fly.

Conclusion

This case study highlights a critical truth for commercial drone operators: the legal risk from a single flight doesn't end when the drone lands. It extends to what happens with the footage — who reviews it, who publishes it, and whether the people captured in it ever consented to being filmed.

Australia's legal framework for drone privacy is fragmented across federal aviation rules, state surveillance legislation, and civil common law. That complexity doesn't reduce your exposure — it increases it. A claim doesn't need to succeed in court to damage your business. The cost of a complaint, investigation, or civil dispute can be significant.

The simplest protection is professional discipline: plan every job, review every frame, and never assume a client will handle privacy compliance on your behalf.
