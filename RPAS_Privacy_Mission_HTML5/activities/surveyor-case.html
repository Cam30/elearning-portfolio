Case Study

The Boundary
Survey Incident

A lawful commercial survey flight — and the privacy exposure that follows when the camera captures more than the cadastral boundary.

📋 Incident Summary

You are engaged to conduct an aerial boundary survey of a residential property in dispute. You fly to capture cadastral reference footage.

During the flight, you incidentally capture the neighbouring property's backyard — including a family using a spa. The neighbour contacts your firm two days later, stating they were filmed without knowledge or consent.

Three Factors That Define the Risk

🏠
Private Premises A backyard is private property — not a public space. Reasonable expectation of privacy is high.
🛁
Private Activity A family using a spa constitutes a private activity — not incidental background presence.
📅
Post-Capture Notice The complaint arrives two days after the flight — the footage already exists and may have been delivered.
💡

A lawful survey flight is not a privacy clearance. CASA authorises the operation of the aircraft. It does not authorise the collection of personal information from neighbouring properties.

Case study: legal breach risk

The legal risk exposure in this case study includes:

1

Privacy Act 1988 — OAIC Enforcement (APP entities)

Where the operator is an APP entity (annual turnover >$3M), the incidental footage engages APP 4 — unsolicited personal information that cannot be collected under APP 3 must be destroyed or de-identified as soon as practicable. APP 11 governs the security of any footage retained pending legal advice. The OAIC may issue a compliance notice, an infringement notice, or commence proceedings for serious interference with privacy under the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024.

2

Statutory Tort — Serious Invasion of Privacy (all operators)

Even where the operator is not an APP entity, the neighbour may sue directly under the statutory tort (commenced 10 June 2025). The key question is recklessness: was capturing the neighbouring backyard a foreseeable consequence of the flight path? A mission plan that does not account for adjacent properties answers that question against the operator. No proof of damage required.

3

State Surveillance Devices Acts (jurisdiction-dependent)

In NSW, s8 of the Surveillance Devices Act 2007 is property-entry based and may apply at low altitude over a neighbouring lot. In Victoria, s7 prohibits recording a private activity to which the operator is not a party — a family in a spa qualifies. WA and NT provide explicit outdoor activity-based protections. The statutory tort now offers a more consistent remedy across all jurisdictions for this fact pattern.

"I was only surveying the client's land" is not a defence. The tort does not require intent to film the neighbours — only that filming them was foreseeable and not addressed in the mission plan.

Practical Guidance

Implications for
Drone Pilots

What this scenario requires of every operator conducting surveys near residential properties.

✓
Survey scope ≠ capture scope Your engagement covers the client's boundary. Your camera does not stop at that boundary. Every mission plan must identify adjacent residential properties and specify how capture will be limited or avoided.
✓
Pre-flight adjacency assessment is mandatory Before flying any residential survey, identify all neighbouring properties within your camera's field of view at planned altitude. Adjust flight path, altitude or camera angle to minimise incidental capture. Document this assessment.
✓
Segregate and restrict incidental footage immediately Any footage capturing neighbouring property must be quarantined on landing — before delivery, before review by the client, before any file transfer. Do not include it in the deliverable. Delete or securely destroy it once the survey is complete.
✓
Respond to a complaint in writing within 24 hours Acknowledge the complaint, confirm the footage has been quarantined, and advise the complainant of the steps being taken. Silence or delay after a complaint increases your legal exposure — particularly under the statutory tort.
✓
Document everything that demonstrates reasonable steps Under the statutory tort, recklessness is the contested threshold. A documented mission plan showing adjacency assessment, camera angle limitations and a no-capture protocol is your primary defence. If it is not documented, it did not happen.
✓
CASA clearance is not privacy clearance A lawful flight under CASA Part 101 does not authorise the collection or retention of personal information from neighbouring properties. Privacy obligations apply independently and are not satisfied by aviation compliance alone.

Conclusion

A compliant flight does not mean compliant capture. Aviation law and privacy law are independent obligations.

The central risk is recklessness under the statutory tort — not intent. Filming the neighbours becomes reckless the moment it was a foreseeable consequence of the flight plan that was not addressed. A mission plan silent on adjacent residential properties does not address it.

Two rules govern every survey job near residential land.

Your survey boundary is not your privacy boundary. The camera captures what is in its field of view — not what is on the client's title.

Documentation is your defence. Reasonable steps taken but not recorded provide no protection.
