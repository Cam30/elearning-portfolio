Step 1 of 4 — Threshold Test
Element 1 · Personal Information
Does the incident involve personal information held by your organisation?
Personal information is any information or opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable — whether true or not. Under the Privacy Act 1988, this includes:

Names · addresses · GPS coordinates linked to a person · identifiable images or video · vehicle plates linked to a person · health information · financial details

As a drone pilot, any footage that captures identifiable people or their property in a way that links to their identity is likely to be personal information. Privacy Act 1988 · s 6(1) definition of personal information
🚁
Your scenario
Drone footage captured for a solar-panel feasibility study shows identifiable faces of patients entering and leaving a regional mental health clinic, along with their vehicle registrations and GPS-tagged entry points to the building.
Outcome — Not an Eligible Data Breach
Not Notifiable
No Personal Information Involved
If the lost or disclosed data contains no personal information — for example, pure geographic survey data with no linked identifiers, anonymised thermal scans, or structural measurements with no individual attribution — the incident does not trigger the NDB Scheme.

However: review your data carefully. GPS coordinates linked to a property owner, vehicle registration, or identifiable footage can convert otherwise neutral data into personal information.
✅ Recommended actions
1Document your assessment and the reasons you determined no personal information was involved.
2Review your data collection practices — could future flights capture personal information incidentally?
3Retain this assessment record for 5 years in case of OAIC inquiry.
Step 2 of 4 — Threshold Test
Element 2 · Unauthorised Access, Disclosure or Loss
Has there been unauthorised access to, unauthorised disclosure of, or loss of that personal information?
The NDB Scheme covers three types of incidents:

Unauthorised access — someone who shouldn't have access views, copies or downloads the data (e.g. an office worker opening your USB)

Unauthorised disclosure — personal information is released to someone who shouldn't receive it (e.g. footage emailed to the wrong client)

Loss — you lose physical or technical control of data in circumstances where unauthorised access is likely (e.g. a USB drive goes missing)

Note: accidental internal access within your own organisation may still count if the person has no authorisation. Privacy Act 1988 · s 26WE — Eligible data breach definition
🚁
Your scenario
The unencrypted SD card containing the raw footage was stolen from the pilot's vehicle overnight. Police were notified but the card has not been recovered. The data is now in unknown hands.
Outcome — Not an Eligible Data Breach
Not Notifiable
No Unauthorised Access, Disclosure or Loss
If personal information remains fully under your authorised control — for example, you discovered a potential breach but confirmed data never left your secure system — the incident is not an eligible data breach at this stage.

Important: if you become aware of new facts that suggest unauthorised access did occur, you must re-assess immediately. The 30-day notification clock starts from when you became, or ought reasonably to have become, aware.
✅ Recommended actions
1Document your investigation findings — what checks confirmed no unauthorised access occurred.
2Review your access controls and storage practices to prevent future exposure.
3Retain your assessment record for 5 years.
Step 3 of 4 — Threshold Test
Element 3 · Likely Serious Harm
Is the unauthorised access, disclosure or loss likely to result in serious harm to one or more individuals?
"Serious harm" includes physical, psychological, emotional, financial, or reputational harm. When assessing likelihood, consider:

Sensitivity of the data — health info, location data, financial details, images of children, or footage near sensitive locations (medical centres, schools) significantly increase risk

Scale — how many individuals are affected? More = higher risk

Who has the data now — a malicious actor poses more risk than an accidental internal disclosure

Ability to misuse it — unencrypted, easily readable data in the wrong hands creates immediate risk

For drone pilots: footage of identifiable individuals on private property, near medical facilities, or linked to GPS home addresses is almost always in the likely serious harm category. Privacy Act 1988 · s 26WG — Likely to result in serious harm
🚁
Your scenario
The footage links identifiable individuals to a mental health facility — effectively disclosing sensitive health information. Disclosure creates clear risk of stigma, discrimination, employment harm, or psychological distress to the people filmed.
Outcome — Not an Eligible Data Breach
Not Notifiable
Serious Harm Not Likely
If the incident involves personal information but the circumstances make it unlikely that any individual will suffer serious harm, it does not meet the NDB threshold.

Example: survey data showing a property boundary with only publicly available information, no images of people, and no sensitive personal details — disclosed to a wrong recipient who immediately deleted it.

Important: the "likely" test is a low bar. If there is a real and not remote chance of serious harm, err on the side of notifying.
✅ Recommended actions
1Document your harm assessment — what factors led you to conclude serious harm was unlikely.
2Consider voluntary notification to affected individuals as a trust-building measure even if not required.
3Review and strengthen your data security practices to prevent recurrence.
Step 4 of 4 — Threshold Test
Element 4 · Remedial Action
Have you been able to prevent the likely serious harm through remedial action before it occurs?
Even if all three previous elements are met, the breach is not notifiable if you took effective remedial action that prevented the serious harm from occurring. This is the "escape route" — but it has a high bar.

Effective remedial action could include: remotely wiping a lost device, recovering data before it was accessed, obtaining binding confirmation from the recipient that data was deleted without being viewed.

Crucially — the remedial action must have actually prevented the harm. Attempting to take action but failing does not satisfy this element. Unencrypted data that was already viewed cannot be "un-viewed."

If data was already accessed or copied, or if the device was unencrypted and is missing, remedial action almost certainly cannot prevent the harm. Privacy Act 1988 · s 26WF — Remedial action
🚁
Your scenario
The SD card remains missing. It was unencrypted. There is no remote-wipe capability and no backup mechanism to recover it. The information is already in circulation — no remedial action can prevent the harm.
Outcome — Eligible Data Breach Confirmed
Notifiable
This IS an Eligible Data Breach.
You Must Act Now.
All four elements of the NDB threshold test are met. This is an eligible data breach under the Privacy Act 1988 and you have mandatory reporting obligations.

The 30-day clock begins from when you became aware — or ought reasonably to have become aware — that an eligible data breach had occurred.
⏱ 30-day notification deadline — clock is running
📋 Your mandatory obligations
1Contain the breach immediately. Take all available steps to limit further access or disclosure — recover devices, revoke credentials, isolate systems.
2Assess the breach. Within 30 days, complete a full assessment: what data was affected, who was involved, what harm could result, and what remedial action has been taken.
3Notify affected individuals. Contact each person whose personal information was involved. Explain what happened, what data was involved, and what steps they can take to protect themselves.
4Notify the OAIC. Lodge an NDB report at oaic.gov.au. Include your assessment, the data involved, the number of affected individuals, and your response steps.
5Document everything. Keep detailed records of your discovery, assessment, notifications sent, and response actions. The OAIC may request this documentation.
Failure to notify: Civil penalties under the Privacy Act can reach $3.32 million for organisations per serious interference with privacy. The OAIC can also make determinations, conduct audits, and require a remediation program.
Outcome — Remedial Action Successful
Not Notifiable
Effective Remedial Action Taken
Although all other elements of the NDB threshold were met, effective remedial action has prevented the serious harm from occurring. This incident is not a notifiable data breach — but your obligations are not over.

Example: An encrypted drive was stolen but the passphrase was never compromised and the drive was never decrypted. The data remained protected throughout.
✅ Required actions even without notification
1Document your assessment thoroughly. Record every step of your investigation and the evidence that harm was prevented. If the OAIC investigates, this is your defence.
2Consider voluntary notification. Even if not required, informing affected individuals demonstrates transparency and protects your professional reputation.
3Review what went wrong. Remedial action succeeding this time doesn't mean your security practices are adequate — fix the vulnerability that created the risk.
4Update your incident response plan. Record what remedial actions worked so you can respond faster in future.
Note: If you later discover that remedial action was less effective than believed — for example, evidence emerges the encrypted drive was cracked — you must immediately re-assess and notify if the threshold is now met.
