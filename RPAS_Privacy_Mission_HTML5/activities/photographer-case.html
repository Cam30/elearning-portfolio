Case Study

The Advertising
Campaign Incident

A commercial drone campaign across three urban locations — and the layered legal exposures that arise when the brief demands real, unscripted crowd footage.

📋 Incident Summary

Your production company is engaged by a major beverage brand to produce a drone-assisted advertising campaign across three urban locations: a rooftop venue, a beach promenade, and a busy CBD laneway.

The brief requires 'authentic crowd energy' — identifiable members of the public reacting to the product. Paid talent is on set, but the client insists the background crowd must be 'real and unscripted'. No consent is obtained from background individuals.

Three Compounding Risk Factors

🎯
Intentional Capture Unlike incidental capture, the brief specifically requires identifiable real people. This is deliberate collection — not inadvertent.
📣
Commercial Publication Footage of unconsenting individuals will appear in a paid advertising campaign — implying product affiliation without consent.
📍
Three Locations Each location — rooftop, beach, laneway — has separate permission, permit, and privacy obligations that stack independently.
💡

A client brief does not create a legal permission. The instruction to capture 'real and unscripted' crowd footage does not override your obligations under privacy law or the Australian Consumer Law. Those obligations transfer to you the moment the drone is in the air.

Case study: legal breach risk

The legal risk exposure in this case study includes:

1

Privacy Act 1988 — Intentional Collection Without Consent (APP Entities)

Where the production company is an APP entity (annual turnover >$3M), deliberately capturing identifiable members of the public for commercial advertising engages APP 3 — personal information may only be collected if reasonably necessary and by lawful, fair means. Collection of identifiable images for brand advertising without consent is not reasonably necessary for any function that justifies bypassing that requirement. The OAIC may issue a compliance notice, an infringement notice, or commence proceedings under the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024.

2

Statutory Tort — Serious Invasion of Privacy (All Operators)

Any identifiable individual appearing in the published advertisement may sue directly under the statutory tort for serious invasion of privacy (commenced 10 June 2025). The tort applies regardless of APP entity status. Unlike earlier case studies where recklessness was the contested threshold, this scenario involves intentional capture — the brief explicitly required real, identifiable people. Intentional conduct satisfies the fault element directly. No proof of damage is required.

3

Australian Consumer Law — Implied Endorsement (s18 & s29 ACL)

Publishing footage of identifiable, unconsenting members of the public in a brand advertisement may constitute misleading or deceptive conduct under s18 of the ACL if a reasonable person would infer those individuals endorse or are affiliated with the product. Where the conduct also amounts to a false representation about endorsement or affiliation, s29 is engaged — which carries significant civil penalties. S18 and s29 are commonly pleaded together. Liability runs primarily to the brand client as the advertiser. The production company may be exposed as an accessory where it knew the essential facts that rendered the conduct misleading — that is, that identifiable unconsenting individuals would be featured in brand advertising implying their affiliation.

The production company delivers the footage as shot — identifiable crowd, no releases, no blurring. The advertisement goes live. What happens next?

1

Privacy Act — OAIC Investigation

A complainant lodges a report with the OAIC. The production company, as an APP entity, cannot demonstrate that collection of identifiable crowd footage was reasonably necessary or consented to. A compliance notice is issued requiring the footage to be withdrawn and a remediation plan filed. Infringement proceedings follow.

2

Statutory Tort — Direct Claim

An identifiable individual recognised in the advertisement commences proceedings under the statutory tort. The capture was intentional — the brief required it. The fault element is satisfied immediately. The claimant does not need to prove harm. Injunctive relief halts the campaign pending resolution.

3

ACL — Implied Endorsement Complaint

A complaint is lodged that unconsenting individuals appear to endorse the brand. The ACCC investigates under s18 and s29 of the ACL — commonly pleaded together where conduct is both misleading and constitutes a false representation about endorsement. The production company's accessorial exposure turns on whether it knew the essential facts — that identifiable unconsenting people would be featured implying affiliation. The campaign is pulled. Remedies and penalties are assessed against the brand client.

Practical Guidance

Implications for
Drone Pilots

What this scenario requires of every operator accepting a commercial advertising brief.

✓
Push back on briefs that require unconsented crowd capture A client instruction to film 'real and unscripted' identifiable members of the public for brand advertising is not operationally neutral. Raise the legal issue before the shoot, not after. If the client insists, require written indemnity covering privacy and ACL claims before your drone takes off.
✓
Obtain location-specific permissions for each site A rooftop venue, beach promenade, and CBD laneway each require separate authorisation — venue owner consent, council filming permit, and CASA clearance. These are independent obligations. CASA clearance does not satisfy council permit requirements, and neither satisfies privacy law.
✓
Use signage and opt-out protocols at each location For shoots in public spaces, prominent signage notifying bystanders that filming is occurring for commercial purposes — with a clear opt-out process — is the minimum best practice standard. It does not eliminate exposure but documents a reasonable step taken.
✓
Do not deliver footage featuring identifiable unconsenting individuals for broadcast Your obligation to review footage before delivery applies here as it does in every other case study. Flag all frames showing identifiable background individuals and require the client to obtain releases or blur faces before publication. Do not make that editorial decision yourself.
✓
Ensure your contract allocates ACL and privacy liability explicitly Your service agreement must specify who is responsible for obtaining consent, who bears liability for publication decisions, and who indemnifies whom in the event of an ACL or privacy claim. A verbal brief does not protect you. A contract that is silent on these points does not either.
✓
Scale your public liability coverage for multi-location commercial shoots Three urban locations, a major brand client, and intentional crowd capture is a high-density, high-exposure operation. Review your coverage limit before the shoot — not after a claim is filed.

Conclusion

A client brief is not a legal authority. The instruction to capture 'authentic crowd energy' does not create consent, does not satisfy APP 3, and does not insulate the production company or the operator from the statutory tort or the ACL.

This scenario is legally distinct from earlier case studies because the capture is intentional. There is no recklessness argument to navigate — the fault element of the statutory tort is satisfied the moment the drone films identifiable people at the client's direction for commercial use without their consent.

Two rules govern every commercial advertising brief.

The brief defines the creative — not the legal permissions. What the client wants and what the law permits are separate questions. Resolve both before launch.

Consent is not an afterthought. For identifiable individuals in commercial advertising, it is a pre-production requirement — not a post-production problem to blur away.
